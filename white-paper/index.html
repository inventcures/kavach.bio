A Respiratory Protection Stockpile for India

Why India needs one, what other countries learned, and a pilot design for Kerala

Working draft v0.1, 18 November 2026. For discussion between tp53 (Ashoka University) and Samarth Srinivas.

1. Executive summary

India should build a respirator reserve for its essential workers, starting with a state pilot in Kerala in 2027, and it should be designed around the one lesson every country’s experience teaches: stockpiles fail through replenishment, not through purchase. The US, UK, Canada, Australia and the EU all bought large stocks and then let them expire, because no one owned the recurring cost of keeping them fresh. India has the advantage of starting now, with that evidence in hand.

Our recommendations:

  1. Protect health workers first, then other frontline workers. India’s own COVID-19 vaccine priority list counted about 1 crore health workers and 2 crore frontline workers1. That 3 crore figure is a defensible, government-sourced sizing anchor.
  2. Make reusable elastomeric respirators the core of the reserve for health workers, with a smaller rotating stock of disposable N95s held through suppliers and hospital purchasing. Elastomerics give higher protection, occupy far less space and cost less per protected day once a crisis lasts beyond a few months. They also need cleaning, training and filter logistics, which must be tested in Indian conditions before buying at scale.
  3. Write rotation and replenishment into the design from day one. Stock should be cycled through routine hospital use, a named body should own the replacement budget, and inventory should be reported every year.
  4. Pair the reserve with a standing surge agreement with Indian manufacturers, triggered by a domestic declaration (a notified disaster under the DM Act) or a WHO emergency declaration, and sized to what routine buyers can absorb.
  5. Start with a 12-month Kerala pilot of about ₹4 crore, or roughly US$470,000 (inferred) covering roughly 5,000 health workers in the Nipah-affected districts. It would test fit, comfort in heat and humidity, cleaning, rotation through the state medical services corporation, and a delivery drill with the state disaster authority.
  6. Treat stock locations as sensitive information, and build procurement integrity in from the start (pre-qualified suppliers, test-on-receipt, no unsecured advance payments, published awards), because the largest pandemic losses in peer countries came from emergency procurement, not from shortages alone.

For scale (inferred): an elastomeric reserve covering India’s health workers for a 150-day emergency would cost on the order of ₹2,600 to 4,200 crore up front, or roughly ₹300 to 500 crore a year over a ten-year facepiece life. That is around 1 to 1.5% of the annual state disaster funds set by the 15th Finance Commission2.

2. The problem

India has met respiratory outbreaks without a reserve

During the 2018 Nipah outbreak in Kozhikode, field staff were at first told to wear “three normal masks at a time”, only a few N95s were available, and some health inspectors were told to buy their own gear with local-body funds3. A private donor airlifted ₹30 lakh of N95s and protective gear about two weeks into the outbreak4. The 2018 outbreak had 23 cases and a 91% case fatality rate, and Kerala has had Nipah spillovers in most years since, including hospital transmission in 20235.

In early 2020 India had no PPE stockpile. It banned PPE exports, made HLL Lifecare the sole national buyer, and planned for 6 million N95s through May6. By April an empowered group estimated a need for 2.7 crore N95s by June7.

Indian industry scaled fast, but not fast enough to substitute for a reserve

Domestic N95 capacity went from about 2.5 lakh a day in mid-May 2020 to 32 lakh a day, with more than 200 makers8. That is a real achievement. But if India’s roughly 1 crore health workers each used one N95 a day, daily need would be about three times that peak capacity, and about ten times if all 3 crore frontline workers are included (inferred). Surge production arrives weeks to months into an emergency, which is too late for the first phase of a fast-spreading respiratory pathogen. A reserve covers the gap until surge capacity arrives.

Quality was also a problem. Police in Bengaluru seized 12,300 fake N95s made from vest fabric in March 20209, and in 2021 HLL caught a shipment of substandard KN95s with forged inspection certificates and encashed a US$9.75 million bank guarantee10.

The policy obligation exists on paper, without money or an owner

India’s plans already tell governments to stockpile, but none says how much, who pays, or who replaces expired stock. Section 3 sets this out. No national PPE or respirator stockpile exists or has been formally proposed. Calls for one have come only from think tanks11. ORF notes that the central and state stocks built in 2021 were short-term, and “there is no information about whether any of these stockpiles continue to exist”.

Three more findings from the high-income evidence bear directly on design.

Stored N95s mostly keep working, but straps fail and storage conditions matter. NIOSH tested about 4,000 stockpiled N95s made between 2003 and 2013. Most models still met standards, and failures clustered at warehouses without temperature or humidity control36. NIOSH separately warned that head straps degrade37. 3M rates its N95s for five years if kept between -20°C and +30°C at no more than 80% relative humidity38. Much of India exceeds both limits for months each year, so climate-controlled storage or validated hot-and-humid shelf-life data is a design requirement (inferred).

A delivered respirator is not the same as a protected worker. When the US stockpile shipped elastomeric respirators to 49 organisations in 2021, only about 31% of the units received were handed out to workers. Training and fit testing took about 30 minutes a person against about 10 for an N9539. Hospitals also tend to fit-test staff on only a few models: UNC Health stocked four N95 models and approved only 7 of 24 donated models it screened40. In 2009, delivered stock was often not the models staff had been fit-tested on41.

Fit depends on who is wearing the mask. In UK hospitals early in COVID, staff from Black, Asian and minority ethnic backgrounds failed FFP3 fit tests 25.7% of the time, against 16.8% for others, and failure rates by model ranged from 2% to 67%42. The choice of models in a stockpile decides who is protected. This is the subject of the companion fit study.

WHO’s guidance supports reusable elastomeric respirators as an option equal to or better than N95s, with caveats on cost, maintenance, storage and the lack of source control in valved models57. Evidence on elastomerics in lower-income settings is thin. The only study we found is a qualitative one from Mali, which concluded they “may be a sustainable and economic solution”58. This is a gap an Indian pilot would fill.

6. Six design lessons

  1. Give replenishment a named owner and its own budget line. Every high-income failure above was a failure to replace expiring stock. India’s reserve should separate the capital cost of building it from the recurring cost of keeping it, and the recurring cost should sit with one body.
  2. Rotate stock through routine use, sized to what routine buyers actually consume. Static deep storage leads to expiry, relabelling and incineration. Fixed offtake contracts that exceed routine demand lead to write-offs, as in Ontario. The UK now calls the working model “dynamic stockpiling”59.
  3. Choose models by fit testing on the people who will wear them. Buy several models and sizes, coordinated with the models hospitals already fit-test on.
  4. Fund the people and procedures, not just the boxes. Training, fit testing, cleaning stations and a respiratory protection programme at the receiving hospital decide whether stock is used.
  5. Build procurement integrity in peacetime. Pre-qualify suppliers, test every lot before payment, avoid unsecured advance payments and referral lanes for connected suppliers, and publish awards.
  6. Rehearse the supply chain, not just the clinical response. The UK’s 2016 pandemic exercise did not test PPE supply at all60.

7. A proposed design for India

7.1 Who is protected first

The strategy behind respirator stockpiling, as set out by Coefficient Giving’s biosecurity team, is that once a dangerous respiratory pathogen is detected, non-essential workers stay home and essential workers wear good respirators. Because a respirator filters air both in and out, exposure falls roughly by the square of the protection factor, so a group protection factor of 30 cuts transmission about 900-fold61. The reserve only needs to cover essential workers, not the whole population.

A worked example of that square rule: if a respirator lets in 1 in 30 particles (protection factor 30) and the infected person’s respirator also lets out only 1 in 30, then only 1 in 900 particles makes the journey from one person’s lungs to the other’s. Two people each wearing a mask that blocks half the particles (protection factor 2) get only a 4-fold reduction.

We propose three tiers (inferred, for discussion):

2 Frontline: police, armed forces, home guards, civil defence, municipal and sanitation workers About 2 crore Same COVID priority list. Civil police actual strength about 14.1 lakh64
3 Other essential services: power, water, food supply, transport, telecom Not yet estimated An occupation-based method like RAND’s US analysis, which gives 5.3 to 85.7 million US workers depending on assumptions, could be applied to India’s labour-force survey data65

The 2 to 13 crore (20 to 130 million) range discussed on our 16 November call has no published source that we could find. We suggest using the government-sourced 3 crore for Tiers 1 and 2 and building Tier 3 with an explicit occupational method.

Our recommended mix (inferred). Tier 1 gets EHMRs held at or near the hospitals that will use them, plus a 30-day rotating buffer of N95s held through vendor-managed inventory with state medical services corporations, so that the N95s are consumed by routine hospital use before they expire. Tier 2 starts with a rotating N95 buffer and moves to EHMRs as the pilot evidence comes in. Build up over 5 to 10 years, which also gives Indian manufacturers a steady order book.

7.3 Where to hold it

There is a spectrum from one central depot to stock held by each worker. The evidence favours spreading stock out (inferred):

  • At the point of use, inside hospitals and the new PM-ABHIM critical care blocks, so EHMRs are already fitted and issued to named staff before an emergency.
  • At district or state level, in state medical services corporation warehouses and Indian Red Cross Society warehouses. The Red Cross already pre-positions relief stock and has a quasi-government governance structure, with the President of India as its president and the Union Health Minister as chair, but it currently holds no PPE76.
  • With suppliers, under vendor-managed inventory contracts with priority access in an emergency, as the EU is now exploring77.

Every storage site needs temperature and humidity monitoring, given the NIOSH finding that failures clustered in uncontrolled warehouses.

Security. A few large depots whose locations are publicly known are an obvious target in a deliberate attack, and production lines are exposed to cyberattack78. India’s existing inventory, the India Disaster Resource Network run by the National Institute of Disaster Management, says only authorised government officers can see uploaded data79. Samarth reported seeing site-level detail such as named metro stations on a public government page, so we should check which system that was and what it exposes before raising it with officials (unverified). Recommendations: keep site-level data access-controlled, publish only aggregate stock levels (months of cover by state), spread stock across many sites, and keep a paper fallback for drills.

7.4 Keeping it fresh

This is where stockpiles fail, so the design should be specific (inferred):

  • A replacement budget separate from the build budget, ideally a fixed share of state disaster mitigation funds or a ring-fenced line in the state health budget.
  • First-expiry-first-out rotation into routine hospital consumption. Kerala’s state medical services corporation was found to have broken this rule in 30 of 67 audited hospitals80, so rotation needs monitoring, not just a rule.
  • Buy a fixed share of the target each year. A CDC team proposed buying 20% of the target every year for a 5-year shelf life, so stock turns over continuously81.
  • Sell or donate near-expiry stock to industrial users, NGOs or neighbouring countries at a discount, as Samarth suggested. Demand in normal times will limit this, so it should be a minor channel.
  • An annual public inventory report (aggregate only), as the UK Inquiry now recommends.
  • Strap and filter checks on a sample of stored units each year.

7.5 Surge manufacturing and an advance commitment

A reserve buys time, and surge manufacturing takes over once it is running. Manufacturers will only invest in standby capacity if they believe the government will buy when the time comes. Lessons from abroad:

  • US federal spending of about US$1.5 billion on mask plants was followed by about 70% of new firms closing and output falling over 90% by 2023, because demand vanished82. Canada saw the same pattern83.
  • Canada’s 3M Brockville deal is a working offtake model: CA$46.6 million of public money for a plant, with each government buying 25 million N95s a year for five years84. But Ontario’s experience shows offtake volumes must match what routine buyers can use.
  • We found no example anywhere of a PPE contract with a trigger written to a declaration. Blueprint recommends that early detection should trigger industry surge, with government guaranteeing to buy the excess if no pandemic follows85.

A model surge commitment for India (inferred, for discussion):

  • Peacetime: a small standing order from each pre-qualified maker, sized to routine hospital demand, which keeps lines running and feeds rotation.
  • Trigger: either a domestic declaration or a WHO declaration, whichever comes first. India notified COVID-19 as a disaster for disaster-fund purposes on 14 March 2020 (unverified, title of circular only), and MHA later let states spend up to 50% of their disaster response funds on COVID, explicitly including PPE86. Domestic triggers can move faster than WHO. A WHO public health emergency of international concern, or the “pandemic emergency” category added by the 2024 amendments to the International Health Regulations (unverified, from background knowledge), could serve as a second trigger.
  • On trigger: the maker scales to a pre-agreed volume within a set number of days, and the government commits to buy up to a ceiling at a pre-agreed price. If the emergency ends early, unused output goes into the rotating reserve rather than to waste.
  • Several makers, not one, so that one acquisition or conflict of interest cannot break the supply. The US ventilator contract history is often cited as a warning on this (unverified).
  • Pre-agreed rules on exports. India’s 2020 export bans and quotas are a recent precedent87. A domestic reserve reduces the pressure to ban exports in the next crisis, which matters for partner countries that depend on Indian supply.

A full term sheet for this commitment is planned as a separate document.

7.6 Distribution and use

  • Issue EHMRs to named staff in peacetime, so fit testing, training and storage are already done. This avoids the US experience where only 31% of shipped units reached workers.
  • Fit test on Indian faces. Stock several models and sizes, including small sizes for women. The companion fit study will set out how.
  • Train in putting on and taking off, cleaning and filter changes, and repeat it in drills. Bangladesh’s experience shows training failures kill too.
  • Distribution for Tier 2 and 3 can draw on existing networks: district health stores, the Indian Red Cross, India Post, pharmacies, and private logistics. Taiwan and Korea showed that national-ID-linked pharmacy rationing plus open stock data works88. Any India version needs privacy safeguards (inferred).
  • Stock visibility. A simple phone-based stock report, as South Africa built in eight weeks, can feed the new statutory disaster database.

7.7 Procurement integrity

Specific rules (inferred, drawn from section 5):

  • A peacetime list of pre-qualified suppliers with BIS IS 9473 certification or NIOSH approval. IS 9473:2002 follows the European FFP1/2/3 classes89.
  • Test a sample of every lot on receipt, before payment.
  • No advance payments without a bank guarantee or escrow.
  • Publish every award within days.
  • Central rate contracts that states must use, so emergency waivers cannot bypass them.

The Comptroller and Auditor General found that Kerala’s medical services corporation paid up to ₹1,550 for PPE kits against an approved ₹545, with a 100% advance, under a tender exemption in March 202090. A pilot with transparent procurement would answer that finding directly.

8. A Kerala pilot

Why Kerala

  • Recurrent high-fatality outbreaks with respiratory transmission risk: Nipah outbreaks in 2018, 2019, 2021, 2023, 2024 and 2025, including hospital spread in 202391.
  • Institutions already in place: the state disaster authority declared COVID-19 a “state calamity” on 3 February 202092; the Institute of Advanced Virology now tests for Nipah locally93; the state adopted an epidemic management plan in 202694; and the state medical services corporation already buys N95s.
  • A documented problem statement: the 2018 field shortage and the audited 2020 PPE purchases.

Nipah outbreaks need thousands of respirators, not crores, so the pilot is a test of the system (fit, use, rotation, delivery, governance) rather than a stock sized for Nipah alone (inferred).

Scope (inferred, for discussion)

  • About 5,000 health workers in Kozhikode and Malappuram: government tertiary and district hospitals, primary health centres in Nipah-affected blocks, and field surveillance teams.
  • EHMRs issued to named staff in high-exposure roles, with filters and cleaning stations.
  • A rotating buffer of about 2 lakh N95s held under vendor-managed inventory with the state medical services corporation, consumed by routine use.
  • One private hospital chain or medical college as a comparison site, if willing.

What it tests

Question Measure
Do Indian health workers pass fit tests with the stocked models? First-attempt quantitative fit-test pass rate by model and size, by sex (with the companion fit study)

9. Funding and institutional home

Who might fund this

  • Coefficient Giving (formerly Open Philanthropy). Its 2026 biosecurity request for proposals sought country-level PPE stockpiles for key workers and engagement of philanthropists to fund them. The round closed on 11 May 2026, but the page invites unsolicited proposals95. One of its team’s ten priority projects is “PPE stockpiles and emergency distribution in X country”, with a goal of at least three countries with 500 million or more people covering all critical workers for six months by end-202796. India alone would meet the population condition (inferred).
  • Blueprint Biosecurity, which has funded a Singapore workshop on next-generation PPE policy for East and Southeast Asian governments97 and is in contact with Samarth.
  • Indian CSR. Schedule VII of the Companies Act covers preventive health care (item i) and disaster management (item xii), and the Ministry of Corporate Affairs confirmed in 2020 that contributions to state disaster management authorities count98.
  • Indian philanthropy. COVID precedents include Tata Trusts and Tata Sons (₹1,500 crore), Azim Premji Foundation and Wipro (₹1,125 crore), and ACT Grants’ ₹10 crore for N95 capacity at Arvind99. We have not found PPE-specific commitments from Piramal, Cipla Foundation, Rohini Nilekani Philanthropies or the Gates Foundation (unverified).
  • Government co-funding. The 15th Finance Commission set state disaster funds of ₹1.6 lakh crore for 2021-26, with 20% reserved for mitigation100. A state that adopts the pilot could fund replenishment from its mitigation window (inferred).

10. Risks and open questions

  • Comfort and acceptance. Elastomerics are heavier and hotter than N95s, and Indian summers and monsoons may cut wear time. The pilot exists to find out.
  • Filter ageing in heat and humidity is unknown until the Blueprint-funded studies report in mid-2027.
  • No Indian elastomeric manufacturing may mean early imports. A domestic production pathway should be part of the scale-up plan.
  • Political durability. The replacement budget will face the same pressure that ended California’s reserve. Building rotation into routine hospital buying reduces how much budget is needed.
  • Pathogen fit. Respirators protect against airborne and droplet spread. Nipah spreads mainly through contact and droplets, and WHO advises airborne precautions only in some circumstances, so the reserve’s case rests on a future airborne pathogen, with Nipah as the practical test bed.
  • Procurement capture. Any new public budget line attracts the failures in section 5. The integrity rules in 7.7 are as important as the stock.

Appendix A. Verification notes

Things that need checking before this paper is shared:

  • The exact wording and responsibility rows of NDMP 2019 section 7.15. Our research could not extract the PDF, so we rely on ORF and Carnegie India quoting it.
  • Which government website shows stock locations publicly. IDRN says its data is restricted to authorised officers.
  • Indian bulk prices for N95s, EHMRs and filters. All cost figures in this draft use US prices and assumptions.
  • Whether any Indian firm makes elastomeric respirators at scale, and their BIS certification.
  • The 14 March 2020 notification of COVID-19 as a disaster (we saw only the circular title).
  • The 2024 IHR “pandemic emergency” category and India’s position on the amendments.
  • The US ventilator contract history (Newport, Covidien, Philips), if we cite it.
  • Whether Magnum Health & Safety, 3M India or Honeywell hold NIOSH approvals for Indian-made respirators.
  • Blueprint’s grant amounts from Coefficient Giving (secondary source only).

Corrections to working assumptions from the 16 November call:

  • Disposable shelf life. Manufacturers rate N95s for about five years under controlled storage, not six months to a year, and NIOSH found most 2003 to 2013 stock still filtered well. Straps and storage conditions are the weak points.
  • Elastomeric shelf life. “Ten years or more” is Blueprint Biosecurity’s claim. No long-term storage study has yet confirmed it.
  • N95 price controls. India never capped N95 prices by statute. Masks were essential commodities from 13 March to 30 June 2020, but N95s were left out of the price caps, and the pricing authority issued only advisories103.
  • Indian Red Cross. It holds relief stock such as hygiene kits, blankets and tarpaulins, but no PPE line in its latest annual report.
  • Critical worker numbers. The 2 to 13 crore range is unsourced. The government’s own 3 crore figure for health and frontline workers is a better anchor.
